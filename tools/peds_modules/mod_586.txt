ID: 23641 | Answer: [A] Galactosemia due to GAL-1-P uridyl transferase enzyme deficiency

OPTIONS: A) Galactosemia due to GAL-1-P uridyl transferase enzyme deficiency | B) Hereditary fructose intolerance due to aldolase B deficiency | C) Glucose-6-Phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency | D) Galactosemia due to galactokinase enzyme deficiency
EXPLANATION: Explanation: A positive Benedict's test with vomiting after milk feeding in a neonate indicates classic galactosemia, due to GAL-1-P uridyl transferase deficiency. Classic galactosemia is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by deficiency of the enzyme galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT). This leads to accumulation of galactose-1-phosphate and galactose in the blood and tissues after ingestion of lactose (milk sugar). Benedict's test may be positive for fructose, but timing of symptoms doesn't match. G6PD deficiency (Option C): Leads to hemolysis, especially after oxidative stress (e.g., infection, fava beans, certain drugs). No vomiting after feeding or positive Benedict's test expected. ID: 23686 | Answer: [B] Calorie deficit with normal serum protein
QUESTION: Out of the following, which represents marasmus accurately?
OPTIONS: A) Loss of protein and presence of edema | B) Calorie deficit with normal serum protein | C) Low cortisol | D) Increased insulin
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Severe calorie deficit with normal serum protein describes the metabolic status of Marasmus. Marasmus represents a state of severe calorie deficiency with normal or near-normal serum proteins . It results from prolonged starvation or chronic illness in which both fat stores and muscle tissue are broken down for energy, causing extreme wasting. The somatic protein compartment (skeletal muscle) is catabolized to supply amino acids, while the visceral protein compartment (including serum albumin) remains largely preserved, so serum albumin levels are usually normal or only mildly reduced. Clinically, the child is extremely thin with pronounced loss of subcutaneous fat. Muscles of the shoulders, arms, thighs, and buttocks are markedly wasted. Loss of the buccal fat pad produces an aged or "monkey-like" facies , and the lax skin of the buttocks gives a " baggy pants" look. Despite the profound emaciation, there is no edema , and the child often appears relatively alert and responsive. Explanations for other Options: Loss of protein and presence of edema(Option A): This describes kwashiorkor , not marasmus. Kwashiorkor results from inadequate protein intake with relative calorie sufficiency, leading to hypoalbuminemia and pitting edema. Low cortisol (Option C): Children with marasmus generally maintain normal or even elevated cortisol levels as a stress response; low cortisol is not characteristic of this condition. Increased insulin (Option D): Severe calorie deprivation causes low insulin and increased counter-regulatory hormones (like cortisol and glucagon) to promote catabolism, so high insulin is inconsistent with marasmus. ID: 23700 | Answer: [B] Fanconi syndrome
QUESTION: A 7-month-old girl presents to your clinic with recurrent episodes of fever, vomiting and chronic constipation associated with poor weight gain. Urinalysis shows a pH of 5.0, specific gravity of 1.010, 1+ protein and 1+ glucose with a normal urine anion gap. Blood work shows a decreased phosphorus and calcium, elevated serum chloride, and a normal glucose and albumin level. Arterial blood gas shows a metabolic acidosis. What would best explain these findings?
OPTIONS: A) Congenital nephrotic syndrome | B) Fanconi syndrome | C) Renal tubular acidosis type 1 | D) Renal tubular acidosis type 4
TAG TOPIC: TUBULAR DISEASES Difficulty level: GOOD TO KNOW Reference: Nelson textbook of pediatrics, 1 st SA ed., pg. 2529
EXPLANATION: This child has symptoms and laboratory studies consistent with Fanconi syndrome, otherwise known as global proximal tubular dysfunction. It can be hereditary or acquired. Inherited forms are secondary to a genetic abnormality, such as galactosemia, Wilson disease and cystinosis. Acquired forms are usually secondary to drugs and substances (e.g., gentamicin, valproic acid, cisplatin, 6-mercaptopurines, lead, mercury, uranium, platinum, and toluene ) through acute tubular necrosis, alteration of renal blood flow, intratubular obstruction and allergic reactions. It is a generalized defect in the proximal tubule leading to: Polyuria: Increased urination. Polydipsia: Increased thirst due to loss of water. Glycosuria: Presence of glucose in the urine despite normal blood glucose levels. Aminoaciduria: Excess amino acids in the urine. Phosphaturia: Increased phosphate excretion, which can lead to bone issues. Metabolic Acidosis: Due to bicarbonate loss. Hence, the syndrome is characterized by low molecular weight proteinuria, glycosuria, phosphaturia, aminoaciduria and proximal renal tubular acidosis. Patients may present with growth failure early in the first year of life, with symptoms secondary to phosphate wasting, such as rickets. Specific laboratory abnormalities include glucosuria, normal blood sugar, high urine pH, mild to moderate serum hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, mild albuminuria, and normal serum protein and albumin. KEY TAKEAWAYS: Fanconi syndrome is a rare disorder characterized by a dysfunction in the renal tubules of the kidneys, leading to a variety of metabolic abnormalities. It primarily affects the proximal renal tubules, resulting in the impaired reabsorption of substances that should normally be reabsorbed from the urine back into the bloodstream.
