ID: 2298 | Answer: [B] Surface tension - increased; Compliance decreased
QUESTION: A preterm infant born at 28 weeks develops signs of respiratory distress syndrome. Which statement accurately describes the changes in surface tension and lung compliance in this condition?
OPTIONS: A) Surface tension - decreased; Compliance - increased | B) Surface tension - increased; Compliance decreased | C) Both surface tension and compliance decreased | D) Both surface tension and compliance increased
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Surface tension increased and compliance decreased; this statement is accurate regarding surface tension and compliance in a preterm baby with respiratory distress syndrome (RDS). Elastic tissue recoil and surface tension are the two opposing forces to lung compliance, and are not opposing forces to each other. Two primary factors influence lung compliance: Elastic forces of the lung tissue : These forces are primarily determined by the presence of elastin and collagen fibres within the lung parenchyma (the functional tissue of the lungs). In a deflated lung, these fibres are contracted and kinked. As the lung expands during inspiration, these fibres stretch and unkink, generating an elastic force that opposes further expansion. Surface tension : A thin layer of fluid lines the inside of the alveoli. At the air-fluid interface within the alveoli, surface tension arises from the stronger attraction between water molecules compared to the attraction between water molecules and air molecules. This surface tension creates an inward pull, attempting to minimise the surface area of the fluid lining the alveoli. Consequently, this inward pull contributes to the elastic recoil of the lungs, making them tend to collapse. Reference: Pulmonary Compliance - StatPearls - NCBI Bookshelf

ID: 2330 | Answer: [D] Type 2 pneumocyte
QUESTION: A preterm neonate develops respiratory distress soon after birth. The pediatrician suspects neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) due to inadequate surfactant secretion. Which type of pulmonary cell is primarily responsible for surfactant production in the lungs?
OPTIONS: A) Type 1 pneumocyte | B) Alveolar macrophage | C) Dust cells | D) Type 2 pneumocyte
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Type 2 pneumocytes are cuboidal cells located in the alveoli. They synthesize and secrete pulmonary surfactant , composed mainly of dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine (DPPC), phospholipids, and surfactant proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D) . Surfactant reduces alveolar surface tension, prevents alveolar collapse (atelectasis), and enhances lung compliance. Surfactant secretion begins around 20-24 weeks gestation and becomes adequate after 34-36 weeks , which explains why preterm neonates are at high risk of RDS. Explanation of Incorrect Options: Type 1 pneumocyte (Option A) : Flat squamous cells covering ~95% of alveolar surface; specialized for gas exchange , not surfactant secretion. Alveolar macrophage (Option B) : Immune phagocytic cells that clear pathogens and debris; no role in surfactant production. Dust cells (Option C) : Another name for alveolar macrophages &rarr; immune defense role only, not surfactant secretion. Key Takeaway points: Clinical Correlation: Neonatal RDS : Caused by surfactant deficiency &rarr; alveolar collapse, hypoxemia, "ground-glass" appearance on CXR. Treated with exogenous surfactant & maternal steroids (Betamethasone) if preterm delivery anticipated. ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome): Secondary surfactant dysfunction due to alveolar damage (sepsis, trauma, aspiration, etc.).

ID: 23339 | Answer: [D] Back blows
QUESTION: After encouraging coughing, what is the next step in the management of a choking infant?
OPTIONS: A) Heimlich manoeuvre | B) Chest compressions | C) Intubation | D) Back blows
TAG TOPIC: RESPIRATORY EMERGENCY Difficulty level: MUST KNOW PYQ: INICET MAY 2022; FMGE JAN 2022 Reference: Manual of paediatric emergencies & critical care, 2 nd ed., pg. 47
EXPLANATION: CHOKING IN INFANTS: In this age group a combination of back blows and chest thrusts is the recommended practice. To begin with five back blows are performed , checking after each one to see if the foreign body has been expelled. Place infant on forearm- face down with head pointing towards the ground. With the hand of the rescuer supporting the jaw (keeping it open). With the free hand five back blows are given. If this does not improve the situation the infant is turned over (still head down) and placed along the rescuer's arm. Then 5 chest thrusts are performed. Two fingers are placed at the lower edge of the sternum (below nipple line). If the infant is too large then they can be placed on the lap of the rescuer. CHOKING IN CHILDREN: If the child is coughing this should be encouraged. Back blows are the first step with the child supported in a leaning forward position. This should be attempted as 5 back blows with hand placement between shoulder blades, checking after each blow to see if the foreign body has been expelled. If this fails the Heimlich manoeuvre should be performed. This is repeated 5 times unless the object is expelled. Place the child on a flat surface (supine). Assess airway for evidence of visible object causing obstruction. Open the airway and given 5 inflation breaths. Then continue the BLS algorithm as per the Basic Life Support Each time breaths are attempted look in the mouth for evidence of foreign body and remove it if visible. If effective breathing commences, place in the recovery position and monitor. ID: 23359 | Answer: [A] Gestational age
QUESTION: You are formulating a differential diagnosis for a newborn infant with respiratory distress. Which of the following is MOST closely associated with the development of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease)?
OPTIONS: A) Gestational age | B) Cesarean section delivery | C) Maternal diabetes | D) Meconium in the amniotic fluid
EXPLANATION: Prematurity is the strongest predisposing factor to surfactant deficiency disease or respiratory distress syndrome (RDS). While low birth weight infants are also at risk, the risk is more related to the gestational age of the infant. Maternal diabetes does predispose newborns to RDS by causing delayed surfactant release compared to infants of comparable gestational age, but is not as strong of a risk as prematurity itself. Cesarean delivery predisposes more to a condition known as transient tachypnea of the newborn, related to excessive fluid retention within the lungs. Meconium in the amniotic fluid may cause respiratory disease if aspirated into the infant's lungs but the pathophysiology of the disease differs from RDS. ID: 23361 | Answer: [B] Lecithin
QUESTION: A newborn presented with chest retractions, dyspnea, and lethargy. The pediatrician diagnosed the baby with respiratory distress syndrome. This occurs due to a deficiency of:
OPTIONS: A) Dipalmitoyl inositol | B) Lecithin | C) Sphingomyelin | D) Dipalmitoylphosphatidylethanolamine
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Lecithin (phosphatidylcholine) is the key surfactant component deficient in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome. Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS), also known as Hyaline Membrane Disease (HMD), is a condition most commonly seen in premature infants born before 34-35 weeks gestation due to a deficiency of pulmonary surfactant. Surfactant is synthesized by type II pneumocytes and reduces surface tension in the alveoli, preventing their collapse during expiration. The primary phospholipid component of surfactant is phosphatidylcholine, commonly known as lecithin, especially in its active form-dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC). Surfactant production starts around 20 weeks of gestation but becomes functionally adequate only after 34-35 weeks. In premature infants, low levels of lecithin lead to alveolar collapse, impaired gas exchange, tissue hypoxia, and formation of a proteinaceous hyaline membrane lining the alveoli. Clinical presentation includes: Tachypnea Chest wall retractions Grunting respiration Cyanosis Decreased air entry Classic X-ray features: Ground-glass opacities due to alveolar atelectasis Air bronchograms Reticulogranular pattern Management involves: NICU admission Supportive care (e.g., IV fluids, oxygen) Surfactant replacement therapy if needed Respiratory support (CPAP or mechanical ventilation) Explanation of other options: Dipalmitoyl inositol (Option A): A phospholipid involved in cellular signaling, not a surfactant component, and not relevant to RDS pathophysiology. Sphingomyelin (Option C): Present in cell membranes and used in the L/S ratio to assess lung maturity, but it does not directly contribute to surfactant function or alveolar stability. Dipalmitoylphosphatidylethanolamine (Option D): Although it is a membrane phospholipid, it lacks the surface tension-reducing ability of dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (lecithin), which is essential for preventing alveolar collapse.
