ID: 1651 | Answer: [C] Scurvy
QUESTION: White line of Frenkel is seen in: September 2008
OPTIONS: A) Osteoporosis | B) Osteomalacia | C) Scurvy | D) Beri-Beri
EXPLANATION: Ans. C: Scurvy Clinical symptoms and signs of infantile scurvy, in order of frequency are irritability, tenderness and weakness of lower extremities, a scorbutic rosary of the legs, bleeding of the gums (usually where teeth have erupted), and fever. Radiographic findings of scurvy: Dense metaphyseal line-This is due to an intensification of the zone of preparatory calcification, resulting from matrix formation failing, is has been referred to as the white line of Frenkel, but is non-specific, as it is also seen in healing rickets, and lead or phosphorus poisoning. Ground glass osteoporosis: This appears at the end of the shaft with blurring or disappearance of trabecular markings. Halo ossification centre: Also called the Wimberger's ring, it is the same effect that produces the white line of Frenkel, affecting the epiphyseal ossification center. Corner sign-Seen due to subepiphyseal infarction, or separation of the epiphysis from the metaphysis. Lateral spurs-These metaphyseal spurs project at right angles to the axis of the shaft, they may be seen due to mushrooming of epiphysis on the metaphysis, or may represent earliest calcification of periosteum elevated by a subperiosteal hemorrhage. Subperiosteal hematomas-These occur at the end of long bones, seen after about 2 weeks of onset of clinical symptoms; it is not the periosteal hemorrhage that calcifies, but the elevated periosteum, secondary to resumption of bone formation. Metaphyseal fractures-Subperiosteal comminuted fractures at the end of long bones, extending only partially through the width of the bone. Atrophy scurvy line-A radiolucent zone on the shaft side of Frenkel's white line, it has been referred to as the Trummerfeld Zone.

ID: 23739 | Answer: [B] Vitamin C deficiency
QUESTION: A child presents with generalized body aches, poor wound healing, corkscrew hairs on her arms, and white lines of Frenkel on X-ray as shown below. This clinical presentation is most consistent with which of the following micronutrient deficiencies?
OPTIONS: A) Zinc deficiency | B) Vitamin C deficiency | C) Copper deficiency | D) Iron deficiency
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) leads to defective collagen synthesis, resulting in fragile capillaries, bleeding, poor wound healing, corkscrew hairs, and characteristic radiological findings like white lines of Frenkel. Vitamin C plays a crucial role in collagen synthesis, which is essential for maintaining the structural integrity of blood vessels, skin, bone, and connective tissues. Scurvy occurs when there is prolonged deficiency of vitamin C, especially in children with inadequate dietary intake (e.g., lack of fruits and vegetables). Clinical Features of Scurvy: Musculoskeletal pain, particularly in legs Corkscrew hairs, petechiae, ecchymoses Poor wound healing, bleeding gums, anemia Pseudoparalysis: refusal to move limbs due to pain Frog-leg posture Scorbutic rosary at the costochondral junctions White lines of Frenkel (dense metaphyseal lines seen on X-ray) Pelkan spurs: metaphyseal spurs Radiological Signs : White line of Frenkel: dense zone of provisional calcification Wimberger ring: circular calcification surrounding the epiphysis Scorbutic rosary: sharply defined, angular swellings at the costochondral junctions (unlike the smoother swellings in rickets) Treatment : Vitamin C supplementation (100-200 mg/day) Symptom resolution typically begins within 1 week Explanation of other options: Zinc deficiency (Option A): Causes growth retardation, alopecia, delayed wound healing, hypogonadism, and dermatitis, but not corkscrew hairs or Frenkel lines. Copper deficiency (Option C): Leads to anemia, neutropenia, and hypopigmented hair, but not the bleeding or radiologic features seen in scurvy. Iron deficiency (Option D): Presents with pallor, fatigue, irritability, and microcytic hypochromic anemia, but not corkscrew hairs, bleeding gums, or radiologic signs of scurvy.

ID: 23878 | Answer: [A] A, D, E
QUESTION: Which vitamins need to be supplemented in CF patients?
OPTIONS: A) A, D, E | B) B complex, C | C) A, B complex, K | D) B complex, C, E
EXPLANATION: Malabsorption is a known complication of cystic fibrosis causing decreased absorption of fat-soluble vitamins. A, D, E and K are fat soluble vitamins and need supplementation in CF .
