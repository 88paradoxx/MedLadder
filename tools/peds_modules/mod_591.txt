ID: 2813 | Answer: [A] Congenital absence of ganglion cells in both myenteric plexus and submucosal plexus of a segment of distant colon
QUESTION: An infant visits a pediatric presenting with symptoms of abdominal distension, anorexia, and lassitude. He was diagnosed with Hirsch sprung disease. What is the cause of Hirsch sprung disease?
OPTIONS: A) Congenital absence of ganglion cells in both myenteric plexus and submucosal plexus of a segment of distant colon | B) Congenital absence of colon | C) Hyperactivity of colon smooth muscles | D) Paralysis of colonic smooth muscles due to trauma during parturition
EXPLANATION: Option A: Some children have a genetically determined condition of abnormal colonic motility known as Hirschsprung disease or aganglionic megacolon, which is characterized by abdominal Distension, anorexia, and lassitude. Distension of segment proximal to aganglionic segment is seen. The disease is typically diagnosed in infancy, and affects as many as 1 in 5000 live births. It is due to a congenital absence of the ganglion cells in both the myenteric and submucous plexuses of a segment of the distal colon, as a result of failure of the normal cranial-to-caudal migration of neural crest cells during development. Option B: (Congenital absence of colon): This would lead to severe gastrointestinal issues but is not the specific cause of Hirschsprung disease. Option C: (Hyperactivity of colon smooth muscles): This would not explain the symptoms; Hirschsprung disease involves an absence of ganglion cells, leading to decreased motility. Option D: (Paralysis of colonic smooth muscles due to trauma during parturition): Trauma can affect nerves, but Hirschsprung disease specifically results from a congenital lack of ganglion cells. Key takeaways: Hirschsprung disease is caused by the congenital absence of ganglion cells in the myenteric and submucosal plexuses of the distal colon, leading to symptoms like abdominal distension and anorexia. Level: Must know Tag: GI motility Reference: Ganong's review of medical physiology, 26th edition, Page 1144

Absence of rectoanal inhibitory reflex suggests Hirschsprung disease. Rectal suction biopsy: The Gold Standard Test for confirming Hirschsprung disease. Calretinin: Used as an alternative when acetylcholinesterase staining is inadequate. Management: Primarily Surgical: Resection of the aganglionic bowel and pull-through of normal bowel. ID: 24048 | Answer: [A] Intussusception
QUESTION: A baby presents with abdominal pain. On examination, a mass is palpable in the left lumbar region. A barium enema is performed, and the image shown below is obtained. What is the most likely diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) Intussusception | B) Volvulus | C) Duodenal atresia | D) Intestinal obstruction
EXPLANATION: Explanation: The presence of abdominal pain, a palpable abdominal mass, and the characteristic "claw sign" on barium enema is diagnostic of Intussusception . Intussusception is the telescoping of a proximal bowel segment (intussusceptum) into a distal segment (intussuscipiens) . It is the most frequent cause of intestinal obstruction in infants between 5 months and 6 years . Types: Ileocolic (most common), colocolic, and ileoileal. Clinical Features: Triad: Severe intermittent abdominal pain "Red currant jelly" stools (blood + mucus) Palpable abdominal mass Radiology: Barium enema &rarr; Coiled spring sign / Claw sign Ultrasound &rarr; Target sign / Doughnut sign Management: Spontaneous resolution occurs in some infants. Hydrostatic reduction (saline/barium enema under fluoroscopy or USG guidance) is preferred. Surgical reduction is indicated if: There is shock, perforation, necrosis Hydrostatic reduction fails Recurrence is frequent Explanation of Other Options: Volvulus (Option B): Presents with abdominal pain, distension, and constipation. Imaging shows the coffee-bean sign or omega sign , not the claw sign. Duodenal atresia (Option C): Presents in neonates with bilious vomiting soon after birth. Imaging shows the double bubble sign , not claw sign. Intestinal obstruction (Option D): Presents with pain, distension, constipation. X-ray shows multiple air-fluid levels ; barium studies are avoided in acute obstruction. Key Takeaways: Intussusception is the most common cause of intestinal obstruction in infants 5 months-6 years. Classic triad: Abdominal pain + currant jelly stools + palpable mass. Radiological signs: Claw sign (barium enema), Target sign (USG).

ID: 24225 | Answer: [A] Observation
QUESTION: A healthy 6-month-old boy is found incidentally to have a duplicated collecting system with no dilation. He has been asymptomatic and has been growing well. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?
OPTIONS: A) Observation | B) Prophylactic antibiotics | C) Stent replacement | D) Surgical repair
TAG TOPIC: CONGENITAL DEFECTS Difficulty level: GOOD TO KNOW Reference: Nelson textbook of pediatrics, 1 st SA ed., pg. 2563
EXPLANATION: About 25% of anomalies recognized prenatally are congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract. These disorders are varied and may be caused by malformations in the renal parenchyma, disruption in renal migration, or abnormalities in the developing collecting system. Duplication of the collecting system is the most frequently seen congenital urinary tract anomaly and may occur as either complete duplication or partial duplication, which is more common. Duplication is likely the result of ureteric bud duplication. Two distinct pelvicalyceal systems and either one ureter or two ureters that converge proximal to the bladder are present in children with partial duplication. Children with complete duplication also have two distinct pelvicaliceal systems but with two ureters. One ureter typically inserts in the trigone area of the bladder, while the second may insert there or elsewhere in the genitourinary system. In girls, it may enter at the bladder neck, urethrovaginal septum, vagina, cervix, uterus, Gartner duct, or a urethral diverticulum. In boys, it may insert into the posterior urethra, prostatic utricle, seminal vesicle, ejaculatory duct, or vas deferens. For patients, like this boy, who are asymptomatic and do not have dilatation or urinary tract infection, treatment is not indicated and the patient may be observed.
