ID: 24231 | Answer: [B] TAPVC
QUESTION: An infant presented with cyanosis. On cardiac catheterization, all four chambers of heart were found to have identical oxygen saturation (SpO2) values. Likely Diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) TGA | B) TAPVC | C) TOF | D) Truncus Arteriosus
EXPLANATION: In TAPVC, also called TAPVR, there is no direct pulmonary venous connection into the left atrium. All 4 pulmonary veins open into the right side of heart directly or indirectly. This produces mixing of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood before or at the level of the right atrium (total mixing lesion ). This mixed right atrial blood either passes into the right ventricle and pulmonary artery or passes through an atrial septal defect (ASD) or patent foramen ovale into the left atrium, which will be the only source of systemic blood flow. Catheterization shows that the oxygen saturation of blood in both atria, both ventricles, and the aorta is similar, indicative of a total mixing lesion. Reference: Nelson 21 st Edition, Chapter 458.7

ID: 24244 | Answer: [C] Atrial septal defect
QUESTION: Which of the following is not a characteristic of the tetralogy of Fallot?
OPTIONS: A) Ventricular septal defect | B) Right ventricular hypertrophy | C) Atrial septal defect | D) Pulmonic stenosis
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Atrial septal defect (ASD) is not part of the classical four features of Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) . The term tetralogy refers to four distinct anatomical abnormalities that arise due to a single developmental error - anterocephalad (upward and forward) displacement of the infundibular septum during embryogenesis. This malalignment causes a large VSD , subpulmonic stenosis , overriding of the aorta , and right ventricular hypertrophy . ASD may occasionally coexist with TOF, but that changes the diagnosis to Pentalogy of Fallot , not classic TOF. Tetralogy of Fallot: Definition: Most common cyanotic congenital heart disease beyond infancy, caused by malalignment of the conal septum leading to a combination of four characteristic defects. Components of Tetralogy of Fallot: Pulmonary Stenosis : (Option D Ruled Out) Narrowing of the pathway from the right ventricle to the pulmonary artery, possibly affecting the infundibulum, valve, or main pulmonary artery. Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD) : (Option A Ruled Out) A large hole between the right and left ventricles, usually misaligned, allowing abnormal blood flow. Overriding Aorta : The aorta is positioned over the septum, receiving blood from both ventricles. Right Ventricular Hypertrophy : (Option B Ruled Out) Thickening of the right ventricle wall due to the extra effort required to pump blood. Clinical features: Cyanosis (central) due to hypoxia; SpO2 Clubbing Polycythemia Cyanotic spells Auscultatory findings: Ejection systolic murmur in the pulmonary area Single S2 (P2 is soft and inaudible) Chest X-ray: Boot-Shaped Heart or 'Cor en Sabot' appearance = Pulmonary oligemia Key Takeaway Points:

ID: 24246 | Answer: [B] Pulmonary infundibular stenosis
QUESTION: Which of the following is seen in tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)?
OPTIONS: A) Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) | B) Pulmonary infundibular stenosis | C) Inter atrial septal defect | D) Transposition of great vessels
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Pulmonary infundibular stenosis is seen in Tetralogy of Fallot. Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is a cyanotic congenital heart disease defined by four classic defects: Severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction Large ventricular septal defect (VSD) Overriding aorta that receives blood from both ventricles Right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) Blood from the right ventricle passes through the obstructed pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery, producing an ejection systolic murmur . Interestingly, the murmur's intensity is inversely proportional to the severity of pulmonary stenosis -more severe obstruction leads to a larger right-to-left shunt and increased cyanosis. Clinical presentation includes dyspnea on exertion, exercise intolerance, and cyanotic spells (tet spells) . These episodes often occur after waking or with exertion, during which the child may cry, become short of breath, lose consciousness, or experience seizures. Children often squat during these spells , which increases venous return and systemic vascular resistance , thereby improving pulmonary blood flow. Examination findings : Auscultation reveals a loud first heart sound, single second heart sound , and an ejection systolic murmur that ends before the single second sound. ECG shows right axis deviation, right ventricular hypertrophy , T-wave inversion in right precordial leads, and occasionally P pulmonale . Chest X-ray demonstrates a boot-shaped heart (coeur en sabot) due to absent main pulmonary artery segment and oligemic pulmonary fields. Diagnosis is confirmed using echocardiography , which visualizes the VSD, right ventricular outflow obstruction, and aortic override. Explanations for other Options: Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) (Option A): TOF causes right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH) due to obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract, not LVH. Inter atrial septal defect (Option C): TOF involves a ventricular septal defect (VSD) ; the atrial septum is typically normal. Transposition of great vessels (Option D): This is a separate congenital heart defect in which the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left ventricle , and is not part of the TOF anatomy . ID: 24247 | Answer: [B] Breath-holding spell
QUESTION: A 15-month-old boy is brought to the urgent care center after an episode during which he stopped breathing, turned blue, became limp, and stiffened. The episode lasted for about 1 minute, after which, he was very sleepy. The boy had been playing in a park when he fell, scraped his knee, and immediately cried. He then became apneic, cyanotic, and limp; lost consciousness. He has had no recent illness. There is no known family history of syncope, sudden death, or epilepsy. Of the following, based on this boy's history, the MOST likely diagnosis is?
OPTIONS: A) Benign myoclonic seizure | B) Breath-holding spell | C) Long QT syndrome | D) Malingering
EXPLANATION: The child had a typical cyanotic variety of breath-holding spell, triggered by pain followed by crying. Breath-holding spells are reflex events provoked by anger, frustration or pain which makes the child cry incessantly. Suddenly, the crying stops at full expiration and the child becomes cyanotic or rarely pale. In some cases, he may lose consciousness and become hypotonic. These events begin are rare before 6 months of age, start around 1 year of age, peak at 2 years and abate by 5 years of age. The typical history of a provoking event and stereotyped pattern of events help to distinguish it from seizures. Parental reassurance & adequate handling of the event should be advised. The child is turned over to one side so that secretions can drain, avoid picking up the child. A brief tactile stimulus is sometimes used to abort the episode, if unduly prolonged.
