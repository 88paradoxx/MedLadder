ID: 24251 | Answer: [C] Exploration of the testis
QUESTION: A 14-year-old boy presents with a sudden onset of severe testicular pain. On examination, the cremasteric reflex is absent. What is the next best step in management?
OPTIONS: A) Venous Doppler | B) Antibiotics | C) Exploration of the testis | D) Wait and watch
EXPLANATION: Explanation: The clinical presentation is classic for testicular torsion : sudden, severe testicular pain in an adolescent with absent cremasteric reflex . Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency because irreversible ischemic damage can occur within 6 hours . The next step is urgent surgical exploration and detorsion , with fixation (orchidopexy) of both testes. Investigations like Doppler may be considered if the diagnosis is uncertain, but in a clear clinical scenario with an absent cremasteric reflex, surgery should not be delayed . Explanation of other options: Venous Doppler (Option A): Doppler ultrasound can demonstrate absent or reduced blood flow to the affected testis, confirming torsion. However, waiting for imaging wastes the critical golden 6-hour window , and testicular salvage depends on immediate surgical exploration , not investigation. Antibiotics (Option B): Used for epididymo-orchitis , where pain is gradual in onset, the cremasteric reflex is preserved, and fever or urinary symptoms are present. In torsion, antibiotics do not relieve ischemia and delay definitive management. Wait and watch (Option D): Observation is dangerous in suspected torsion, as ischemic necrosis can occur in 6-8 hours . Any delay in exploration drastically reduces the chances of testicular salvage.

ID: 24253 | Answer: [D] Ectopic Ureter
QUESTION: A girl child presents with complaints of constant dribbling of urine since birth. She is, however, also able to void voluntarily. What is the likely diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) Posterior Urethral Valve | B) Ureterocele | C) Primary Vesicoureteric Reflux | D) Ectopic Ureter
EXPLANATION: Clues include constant urinary dribbling, with preserved ability to voluntarily pass urine also, in a girl child. This indicates a likely diagnosis of Ectopic Ureter. Female to male ratio is 2-6:1 Ureteral ectopia, or Ectopic Ureters are usually associated with a duplicated collecting system in females. The condition refers to a ureter that drains outside the bladder, often into the vagina or distal urethra. It can produce urinary incontinence characterized by constant urinary dribbling all day, even though the child voids regularly. Treatment in these cases is either partial nephrectomy, with removal of the upper pole segment of the duplicated kidney and its ureter down to the pelvic brim, or ipsilateral ureteroureterostomy, in which the upper pole ectopic ureter is anastomosed to the normally positioned lower pole ureter. These procedures often are performed by minimally invasive laparoscopy with or without robotic assistance. Reference: Nelson 21 st Edition, Chapter 558

ID: 24305 | Answer: [B] Lung
QUESTION: Which is the most common organ for metastasis in a child with Wilm's tumor?
OPTIONS: A) Brain | B) Lung | C) Liver | D) Bone
TAG TOPIC: SOLID TUMOR Difficulty level: GOOD TO KNOW PYQ: FMGE JULY 2023 Reference: Nelson textbook of paediatrics, 21 st ed, page number: 2683
EXPLANATION: Wilms tumor, also known as nephroblastoma , is the most common malignant renal tumor in children . It primarily affects children between the ages of 1 and 5 years and arises from primitive kidney cells. Wilms tumor results from abnormalities in genes responsible for kidney and genitourinary development. Key genetic abnormalities include: WT1 and WT2 gene mutations on chromosome 11p, important in kidney development. WAGR Syndrome: WT1 gene deletion leads to Wilms tumor, Aniridia, Genitourinary abnormalities, and Intellectual disabilities (Retardation). Denys-Drash Syndrome: WT1 mutation is associated with Wilms tumor, gonadal dysgenesis, and nephropathy. Beckwith-Wiedemann Syndrome: WT2 gene abnormality associated with Wilms tumor, macroglossia, hemihypertrophy, and organomegaly KEY TAKEAWAYS: Wilms tumor, also known as nephroblastoma, is the most common malignant renal tumor in children. EXPLANATION: A child aged 2-8 year with nephrotic syndrome & showing good response to steroids is likely to have minimal change disease. ID: 24461 | Answer: [B] 2,3,4
QUESTION: A 14-month-old child has steroid dependent nephrotic syndrome. Look at the 4 statements mentioned below. 1. All live vaccines can be given 2. Killed vaccines can be safely given 3. Sibling should not be given OPV 4. Pneumococcal Vaccine should be given before commencing the treatment with steroids Which of the following statements about the vaccination of this child is true?
OPTIONS: A) 1,2,3,4 | B) 2,3,4 | C) 2,3 | D) 2,4
EXPLANATION: This is a child with steroid dependent nephrotic syndrome & so the child will be on daily steroid therapy. Regarding immunosuppressed children & vaccination, the following points must be kept in mind: A child receiving Prednisolone is considered immunosuppressed if the dose of prednisolone or its equivalent is &ge;2 mg/kg/day or 20 mg/day for 14 days or longer. These children should not receive live vaccines till at least 4 weeks after the last dose of steroid therapy. All killed vaccines are safe to use in these children, but may need to be repeated due to inadequate serological response. Live vaccines may be used in such kids if the total duration of steroid therapy has been less than 14 days Live vaccines may also be used if alternate-day steroids are being administered or if the steroids are being given via inhaled, topical or intra-articular routes. Wherever possible, vaccination should be completed prior to the institution of immunosuppressive therapy. Household contacts, e.g., Siblings should not receive those live vaccines, in which the organism can be transmitted to the patient, e.g., OPV.
