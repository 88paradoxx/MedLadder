ID: 23857 | Answer: [B] Cystic fibrosis
QUESTION: A 6-month-old infant presents with chronic cough, failure to thrive, and recurrent respiratory infections. On examination, there are crepitations in both lungs. A sweat chloride test is ordered. Which of the following conditions is being evaluated using this test?
OPTIONS: A) Phenylketonuria | B) Cystic fibrosis | C) Gaucher's disease | D) Osteogenesis imperfecta
EXPLANATION: Explanation of answer: Sweat chloride levels are elevated in cystic fibrosis, making it a key diagnostic test for this autosomal recessive disorder. Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the CFTR gene located on chromosome 7q32. The CFTR protein functions as a chloride channel in epithelial cells. A defect in this channel leads to abnormal ion transport, causing thickened secretions in the lungs, pancreas, and other organs. The sweat chloride test is the gold standard diagnostic tool for CF. It is performed using pilocarpine iontophoresis, which stimulates sweat production. The sweat is collected and analyzed for chloride content. Interpretation: Chloride >60 mmol/L in children and adults is diagnostic of CF. 30-59 mmol/L is borderline and requires further testing. This elevation occurs due to defective chloride reabsorption in sweat ducts. On the other hand, chloride levels are decreased in pancreatic secretions in CF. Additional diagnostic tools include: Genetic testing for common mutations like F508del. Nasal potential difference (NPD) measurement to assess CFTR function. Fecal elastase to evaluate pancreatic function. Chest imaging to assess pulmonary involvement. Serum electrolytes may show hyponatremic, hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis due to salt loss (known as contraction alkalosis). Transepithelial Intestinal and Rectal Biopsies: These can be used to directly evaluate CFTR function, though they are generally reserved for research or challenging cases. Explanation of other options: Phenylketonuria (Option A): Caused by a deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase. Diagnosed by plasma phenylalanine measurement, not sweat chloride. Gaucher's disease (Option C): A lysosomal storage disorder due to &beta;-glucocerebrosidase deficiency. Diagnosis is made by enzyme assays and genetic testing, not sweat chloride levels. Osteogenesis imperfecta (Option D): A disorder of type I collagen synthesis, diagnosed by genetic testing or collagen studies, not sweat chloride analysis. ID: 24000 | Answer: [C] Scarlet fever
QUESTION: A 5-year-old boy presents with low grade fever, headache and nasal congestion. He has a flushed face and has developed lacy reticular rash on the trunk and external surface of his arms and legs. He also has strawberry-like tongue. Palms and soles are spared. His mother has been ill with low grade fever and joint stiffness and pain. Which of the following is most likely diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) Rubella | B) Measles | C) Scarlet fever | D) Roseola infantum
EXPLANATION: Scarlet fever is an upper respiratory tract infection associated with a characteristic rash, which is caused by an infection with pyrogenic exotoxin (erythrogenic toxin)&ndash;producing GAS in individuals who do not have antitoxin antibodies. The rash appears within 24-48 hour after onset of symptoms, although it may appear with the first signs of illness. It often begins around the neck and spreads over the trunk and extremities. The rash is a diffuse, finely papular, erythematous eruption producing bright red discoloration of the skin, which blanches on pressure. It is often accentuated in the creases of the elbows, axillae, and groin. The skin has a goose-pimple appearance and feels rough. The cheeks are often erythematous with pallor around the mouth. After 3-4 days, the rash begins to fade and is followed by desquamation, initially on the face, progressing downward, and often resembling a mild sunburn. Occasionally, sheet-like desquamation may occur around the free margins of the fingernails, the palms, and the soles. The pharynx is red, the tonsils are enlarged and often covered with a white, grayish, or yellow exudate that may be blood-tinged. There may be petechiae or &ldquo;doughnut&rdquo; lesions on the soft palate and posterior pharynx and the uvula may be red and swollen. In addition, the tongue is usually coated and the papillae are swollen. After desquamation, the reddened papillae are prominent, giving the tongue a strawberry appearance.
