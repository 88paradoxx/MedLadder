ID: 24030 | Answer: [A] Vincristine + Prednisolone
QUESTION: An 18-month-old child presents with hepatosplenomegaly, a hemoglobin level of 7 g/dL, a white blood cell count of 50,000 cells/cu.mm, and petechiae and purpura. What is the recommended drug treatment for this condition?
OPTIONS: A) Vincristine + Prednisolone | B) IVIG | C) L-Asparinigase +Prednisolone | D) Daunorubicin + Prednisolone
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Vincristine + Prednisolone is the standard induction regimen for childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), the most likely diagnosis in this child. The child presents with anemia, thrombocytopenia, leukocytosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and petechiae, which are classic features of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)-the most common leukemia in children. Initial treatment (induction phase) for ALL aims to achieve complete remission and involves: Vincristine (microtubule inhibitor, halts cell division) Prednisolone (a glucocorticoid, induces apoptosis in lymphoblasts) These agents form the backbone of initial therapy, especially in low-risk or standard-risk ALL. Phases of ALL treatment: Induction therapy: Vincristine + Prednisolone (&plusmn; L-asparaginase, daunorubicin based on risk stratification) Consolidation: To eliminate residual disease and CNS-directed therapy Maintenance therapy: To prevent relapse using methotrexate, 6-MP, etc. Supportive care: Includes acyclovir for viral prophylaxis and cotrimoxazole for pneumocystis prophylaxis. Explanation of other options: IVIG (Option B): Used in immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), not in leukemia. It reduces immune-mediated platelet destruction but doesn't treat leukemic cell proliferation. L-Asparaginase + Prednisolone (Option C): While this combination is also used in ALL therapy, it typically forms part of expanded induction or consolidation phases. Vincristine + Prednisolone is more appropriate for initial induction. Daunorubicin + Prednisolone (Option D): This combination is more commonly used in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). In ALL, daunorubicin is reserved for high-risk or poor prognosis subtypes.

ID: 24312 | Answer: [A] Wilm's tumor
QUESTION: A 4-year-old child is brought in with a 14 cm firm, tender swelling in the flank that has been progressively enlarging over the past few weeks. On examination, the child looks generally well but has discomfort due to the mass. The lump is non-mobile, non-fluctuant, and firm, with no overlying skin changes or signs of infection. EXPLANATION: Explanation: Wilms' tumor (nephroblastoma) is a malignant renal tumor that most commonly occurs in children under 5 years of age. The presentation in this case - a large, firm, non-mobile flank mass - is highly suggestive of Wilms' tumor. It is the most frequent kidney malignancy in the pediatric population, with the majority of diagnoses made before age five. Explanation of Other Options: Angiomyolipoma (Option B): These are benign renal tumors usually seen in adults. It rarely affects children, and both the age of the patient and the clinical features make it an improbable diagnosis in this scenario. Neuroblastoma (Option D): The most common extracranial solid tumor of childhood, typically arising from the adrenal gland. Unlike Wilms' tumor, neuroblastomas often cross the midline and are associated with systemic features such as fever, irritability, weight loss, or paraneoplastic syndromes (e.g., opsoclonus-myoclonus).
