ID: 2047 | Answer: [B] Dystrophin
QUESTION: A 7-year-old boy presented with proximal muscle weakness and low IQ. Gower's sign is present. Which of the following protein (shown in the given figure) will be absent in this case scenario?
OPTIONS: A) Actin | B) Dystrophin | C) Sarcoglycan | D) Laminin
EXPLANATION: Option B: The probable diagnosis is Duchenne muscular dystrophy(DMD). The dystrophin gene is one of the largest in the body and mutations can occur at many different sites in it. Duchenne muscular dystrophy is a serious form of dystrophy in which the dystrophin protein is absent from muscle. It is X-linked recessive disorder and usually fatal by the age of 30. Most common cause of death is respiratory failure. Patients with DMD will show malignant hyperthermia after anasthesia. Partial mutation of dystrophin gene leads to Beckers muscular dystrophy. Diagnostic test is Multiplex ligation probe dependent amplification(MLPDA). Option A: Actin: Actin is a fundamental protein in muscle contraction and is present in all muscle cells. Option C: Sarcoglycans: Sarcoglycans are part of the dystrophin-glycoprotein complex; they may be reduced but are not absent in Duchenne muscular dystrophy. Mutation in gene for sarcoglycan produces limb girdle muscle dystrophy. Option D: Laminin: Laminin is a component of the extracellular matrix and is not absent in muscle diseases like Duchenne muscular dystrophy. ID: 23426 | Answer: [A] Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia
QUESTION: Which of the following is true regarding the mechanism of breast milk jaundice?
OPTIONS: A) Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia | B) Late onset | C) Beta glucronidase enzyme involved | D) Difficulty in excretion of conjugated bilirubin
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Breast milk jaundice typically presents as unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in otherwise healthy, term infants , appearing after the first week of life (late onset).The condition is thought to be caused by beta-glucuronidase in breast milk , which deconjugates bilirubin in the intestine, increasing enterohepatic circulation and slowing bilirubin excretion. Explanations for other Options: Late onset (Option B): Breast-milk jaundice does usually present after day 5-7, so it can be described as "later" than physiologic jaundice . However, the mechanism, specifically involves unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, not just timing. Beta glucronidase enzyme involved (Option C): &beta;-glucuronidase in breast milk indeed contributes by deconjugating bilirubin in the gut, but the primary mechanism is still the resulting unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia. This is a contributing enzyme rather than the core biochemical abnormality. Difficulty in excretion of conjugated bilirubin(Option D): This describes disorders with direct (conjugated) hyperbilirubinemia, such as biliary atresia or neonatal hepatitis, and is not the mechanism in breast-milk jaundice.

ID: 23567 | Answer: [B] Rise in FGF-23 levels
QUESTION: An early marker of Mineral and Bone Disorder in CKD Patient is?
OPTIONS: A) Fall in 1, 25-DHCC levels | B) Rise in FGF-23 levels | C) Fall in FGF-23 levels | D) Rise in PTH levels
EXPLANATION: Chronic kidney disease is characterized by systemic disorders of calcium, phosphorus, PTH, and vitamin D metabolism that can not only lead to bone disorders (renal osteodystrophy) but also vascular and soft tissue calcification. Previously called Renal Osteodystrophy, it is now called as CKD-MBD. Efforts have focused on the role of the hormone fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and its cofactor, Klotho, in CKD-MBD. An elevated FGF23 results in increased urinary phosphate excretion and suppression of 1-&alpha;hydroxylase activity, leading to reduced 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (1,25(OH)2D) values and increased PTH secretion. Elevated FGF23 is the first sign of altered osteocyte function in pediatric and adult CKD, is seen as early as CKD stage 2 (GFR 60-90 mL/min/1.73 m2), and occurs despite normal calcium, phosphorus, PTH, and 1,25OH2 D levels. OPTIONS: A) 6 | B) 4 | C) 12 | D) 10
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Steroid resistance in nephrotic syndrome is diagnosed when there is no remission despite 6 weeks of treatment with daily corticosteroids. Steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome Steroid resistance in nephrotic syndrome is diagnosed when there is no remission despite 6 weeks of treatment with prednisolone (2 mg/kg/day). Can be of 2 types: Primary (initial) steroid resistance, where there is no response to steroids from the beginning Secondary (late) steroid resistance, which develops during relapse treatment. All steroid-resistant patients should undergo a kidney biopsy . Common findings include: FSGS (Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis) or MCD (Minimal Change Disease) Rarely, membranous nephropathy may be seen in older children Genetic Causes of Steroid Resistance : Genetic mutations in genes like NPHS2 (podocin), NPHS1 (nephrin), PLCE1, and others. Next-generation sequencing can help identify these mutations, which are linked to progressive kidney damage and a lower risk of recurrence after kidney transplantation. Management: The treatment focuses on inducing remission of proteinuria while minimizing medication side effects. Drug of choice is Calcineurin inhibitors. Tacrolimus is generally more efficacious and preferred. Cyclosporine is relatively safer if there is severe hypokalemia or seizures. Combination of choice: Calcineurin inhibitors + ACE inhibitors (e.g., Enalapril) or ARBs (e.g., Losartan) + low dose steroids. Low dose steroids (1 to 1.5 mg/kg alternate day or double the maintenance dose) are added for a few weeks for synergistic action and better response, provided there is no steroid toxicity. CNIs are slow-acting, and steroids can help improve outcomes initially.

Down's syndrome Due to Trisomy 21, the result of meiotic nondisjunction in either maternal or paternal gamete. The overexpression of genes located on chromosome 21 contributes to the clinical features. Down syndrome associations: Congenital Heart Disease : 40% of children have congenital heart defects, primarily endocardial cushion defects (40-60% of cases) Commonly associated with AVSD, Ventricular Septal Defect, Atrial Septal Defect, Patent Ductus Arteriosus, and Tetralogy Of Fallot . Hearing Defects : 40-60% experience conductive hearing loss, often due to serous otitis media in the first year. Routine hearing assessments are recommended before 6 months of age and annually. Thyroid Dysfunction : Hypothyroidism occurs in 13-54% of patients. Atlanto-occipital Subluxation : Incidence ranges from 10-30%. A lateral neck radiograph is recommended. Physical Growth : Linear growth is typically slower, and children may become obese with age. Muscle tone often improves, but regular monitoring of height and weight is necessary. Malignancies : Increased risk of lymphoproliferative disorders, such as acute lymphoblastic leukaemia , acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia, and transient lymphoproliferative syndrome. Early-Onset Alzheimer's Disease: The APP gene, located on chromosome 21, plays a significant role in amyloid plaque formation, a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease. OPTIONS: A) Bone marrow transplant (BMT) | B) Methotrexate (MTX) | C) IVIG | D) Aspirin
EXPLANATION: Explanation: First-line treatment for severe thrombocytopenia with bleeding or very low counts is intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) or high-dose corticosteroids to rapidly raise the platelet count. Immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP) is the most likely diagnosis in a child with petechiae, purpura, severe thrombocytopenia (platelets ~10,000/&micro;L), and bone marrow showing increased megakaryocytes , which indicates peripheral platelet destruction rather than marrow failure. Explanations for other Options: Bone marrow transplant (BMT)(Option A): Reserved for marrow failure syndromes (e.g., aplastic anemia, leukemia) or certain congenital disorders, not for typical ITP where the marrow is functioning. Methotrexate (MTX) (Option B): An antimetabolite used in cancer therapy and some autoimmune diseases; it has no role in the acute management of ITP. Aspirin (Option D): It is Contraindicated because it inhibits platelet function and would worsen bleeding risk.

ID: 24387 | Answer: [D] Family history of reading delay
QUESTION: Which of the following is a known risk factor for language delay?
OPTIONS: A) High socioeconomic status | B) Bilingual household | C) Term delivery | D) Family history of reading delay
EXPLANATION: Isolated language delay The cause of language delay is multifactorial. It can be due to biologic factors, such as hearing loss or cognitive difficulties, autism, or environmental causes. Children whose first-degree relatives have a history of language, speech, or reading delays are at greater risk for language disorders. Other known risk factors include premature birth, male gender, and low socioeconomic status. There is no evidence to support a relationship between speech delays and birth order, history of otitis media, or multilingual environments. ID: 24529 | Answer: [B] Needs treatment after 6 years of age
QUESTION: False about thumb sucking among the following is
OPTIONS: A) In adolescence, it is a sign of insecurity | B) Needs treatment after 6 years of age | C) Causes dental malalignment | D) It is a self-soothing behavior
EXPLANATION: Thumb sucking is normal in infancy and toddlerhood, i.e., upto 4-5 years. It is a behavioral problem, which may be self-soothing, i.e., a source of pleasure. Persistent thumb sucking in adolescence is more common in girls and may be sign of insecurity . Persistent thumb sucking in older children can cause dental malalignment. Treatment is not required till the age of 4-5 years. After that behavioral therapy in the form of positive reinforcement (giving reward for not sucking the thumb) is treatment of choice. Negative reinforcement (application of noxious agents to thumb) is second line of treatment.
