OPTIONS: A) 40ml/kg | B) 50ml/kg | C) 60ml/kg | D) 80ml/kg

ID: 24090 | Answer: [C] Extra hepatic biliary atresia
QUESTION: An infant presented with progressive jaundice and pale coloured stools. Liver biopsy revealed bile plugs and bile ductular proliferation. Likely diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) Criggler Najar Syndrome | B) Dubin Johnson Syndrome | C) Extra hepatic biliary atresia | D) Choledochal Cyst
EXPLANATION: The clinical picture is suggestive of neonatal cholestasis. The most common cause of neonatal cholestasis is Extra hepatic biliary atresia (EHBA). Biliary atresia is characterized by bile ductular proliferation, the presence of bile plugs, and portal or perilobular edema and fibrosis, with the basic hepatic lobular architecture intact. In neonatal hepatitis, there is severe, diffuse hepatocellular disease, with distortion of lobular architecture, marked infiltration with inflammatory cells, and focal hepatocellular necrosis; the bile ductules show little alteration. Giant cell transformation is found in infants with either condition and has no diagnostic specificity. Reference: Nelson 21 st Edition, Chapter 383

ID: 24096 | Answer: [D] Brain
QUESTION: A 12-year-old boy seen in your clinic has a history of Wilson disease. Which of the following tissues may accumulate excessive copper if the disease is left inadequately treated?
OPTIONS: A) Skeletal system | B) Pancreas | C) Ovaries | D) Brain
EXPLANATION: Wilson disease is a metal metabolism disorder characterized by excessive copper deposition in the liver, brain, kidneys, and eyes. Features include hepatic failure or cirrhosis, hemolytic anemia, KF rings in the eyes, sunflower cataracts, neuropsychiatric manifestations and parkinsonism-like features In CNS, the excess copper gets deposited into basal ganglia. Patients with Wilson disease may suffer from arthralgias and osteoarthritis but do not typically develop copper deposition in the bones, pancreas, ovaries, or salivary glands. Also note that pancreas and ovaries show deposition with iron in hemochromatosis, but these are not much affected by copper deposits in Wilson Disease. Infants with neonatal iron storage disease typically have excessive iron deposition in the liver, heart, pancreas, and salivary glands. Reference: Nelson textbook of pediatrics, 1 st SA ed., pg. 1939

ID: 24097 | Answer: [C] An infant with preference for carrots and butternut squash can develop toxicity
QUESTION: Which of the following statements about vitamin A toxicity is false?
OPTIONS: A) It can cause pseudotumor cerebri | B) It can cause fissures at the corner of the mouth, pruritis and alopecia | C) An infant with preference for carrots and butternut squash can develop toxicity | D) Symptoms subsides with withdrawal of source
EXPLANATION: Signs of subacute or chronic toxicity can include Headache, vomiting, anorexia dry, itchy desquamating skin fissuring at the corners of the mouth alopecia and/ or coarsening of the hair swelling of the bones enlargement of the liver and spleen diplopia, increased intracranial pressure, irritability, stupor desquamation of the palms and the soles of the feet. Radiographs may show hyperostosis affecting several long bones , especially in the middle of the shafts. Symptoms may subside rapidly on withdrawal of the vitamin , but the rate of improvement depends on the amount of vitamin A that has built up in tissues. In young children, toxicity is associated with vomiting and bulging fontanels. Acute hypervitaminosis A, such as after consumption of a single large (30-60 mg dose) of vitamin A may include nausea, vomiting, and drowsiness; less-common symptoms include diplopia, papilledema, cranial nerve palsies, and other symptoms suggesting pseudotumor cerebri. A syndrome of severe congenital malformations may occur in infants of mothers who have consumed therapeutic doses (0.5-1.5 mg/kg) of oral 13-cis-retinoic acid (e.g., Accutane), generally taken for the treatment of acne or cancer, during the 1st trimester of pregnancy. Excessive intake of carotenoids is not associated with toxicity but can cause yellow coloration of the skin that disappears when intake is reduced ; this disorder (carotenemia) is especially likely to occur in children with liver disease, diabetes mellitus, or hypothyroidism and in those who do not have enzymes that metabolize carotenoids. ID: 24110 | Answer: [C] Up trending total bilirubin
QUESTION: A 10-year-old child with severe acute liver failure is being cared for in the intensive care unit. Which of the following laboratory findings best reflects worsening liver function in this patient?
OPTIONS: A) Up trending AST | B) Up trending ALT | C) Up trending total bilirubin | D) Up trending factor VII
TAG TOPIC: HEPATOLOGY Difficulty level: MUST KNOW PYQ: FMGE DEC 2020 Reference: Nelson textbook of pediatrics, 1 st SA ed, pg no: 1967
EXPLANATION: Rising total bilirubin in the setting of acute liver failure with loss of hepatic function is an ominous sign that is likely multifactorial in nature. This may be due to reduced hepatic uptake, decreased bile conjugation, and/or decreased biliary excretion as hepatocyte death progresses. Serum aminotransferase elevation is usually secondary to hepatocellular injury due to inflammation, toxin, or passive congestion, although there are extrahepatic causes of elevated transaminases as well. Aminotransferases levels may remain stable or trend downward even as bilirubin rises as liver failure and hepatocellular dealt progress.So, up trending AST/ALT do not necessarily reflect loss of hepatic function. Factor VIIis a clotting protein synthesized in the liver that, when levels decline to less than 12% of normal, may predict fulminant hepatic failure.
