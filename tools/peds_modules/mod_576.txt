ID: 2930 | Answer: [B] Fat
QUESTION: Parenteral nutrition is instituted for a 4-day-old infant who has a total bilirubin of 18 mg/dL and a direct bilirubin of 3.0 mg/dL. Which one of the following macronutrients in the parenteral nutrition should be restricted in this patient?
OPTIONS: A) Carbohydrate | B) Fat | C) Protein | D) Vitamin
EXPLANATION: Option B: Reason for Restriction: Infusion of lipids displaces bilirubin from albumin , increasing the risk of kernicterus . Infusion Guidelines: Lipids should be administered at a low rate to prevent essential fatty acid deficiency while minimizing bilirubin displacement. Why not other options: Option A: Carbohydrate: Carbohydrates do not significantly impact bilirubin levels and are necessary for energy, so they should not be restricted. Option C: Protein: Proteins are essential for growth and development, and while they can affect bilirubin indirectly, they are not typically restricted in this context. Option D: Vitamin: Vitamins are necessary for various metabolic processes and are not directly related to bilirubin metabolism, so they do not need to be restricted. Key takeaways: In infant with elevated bilirubin levels, fat should be restricted in parenteral nutrition, as it displaces bilirubin from albumin, increasing the risk of kernicterus. ID: 9377 | Answer: [D] Cabergoline
QUESTION: A G2P1 delivered a preterm baby at 36 weeks. The baby was admitted to the NICU and given expressed breast milk. Unfortunately, the baby did not survive. What is used to reduce pain and engorgement of the lactating breast in this case?
OPTIONS: A) Pramipexole | B) Chlorpromazine | C) Sumatriptan | D) Cabergoline
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Cabergoline is a potent dopamine D2 receptor agonist with minimal activity at D1, 5-HT, and &alpha;-adrenergic receptors. It inhibits prolactin secretion and thereby suppresses lactation, making it useful when lactation must be prevented-for instance, after fetal loss or when breastfeeding is not intended or possible. As the drug of choice for prolactinoma , cabergoline acts specifically on pituitary lactotroph D2 receptors to reduce prolactin release. Its long half-life (approximately 65 hours) allows for lower dosing and better tolerability, which is why it has largely replaced bromocriptine. Explanation of other options: Pramipexole (Option A) and ropinirole are mainly used in the management of Parkinson's disease . They are D2-like receptor agonists with strong affinity for the D3 receptor subtype, though they are less effective than L-DOPA in the early stages of therapy. Chlorpromazine (Option B) , an antipsychotic , can elevate prolactin levels and is therefore inappropriate for inhibiting lactation. Sumatriptan (Option C) is used for migraine treatment and has no influence on prolactin secretion or lactation. Key Takeaways: Cabergoline &rarr; D2 agonist &rarr; inhibits prolactin &rarr; stops lactation. Long-acting, better tolerated than bromocriptine. Avoid dopamine antagonists (e.g., chlorpromazine).

ID: 23383 | Answer: [C] VZIG to baby + Acyclovir to mother as well as baby
QUESTION: What is the therapy of choice in asymptomatic baby born to pregnant female who gets chicken pox one day prior to delivery?
OPTIONS: A) Only Acyclovir to mother and baby | B) VZIG to both mother and baby but no acyclovir to be given | C) VZIG to baby + Acyclovir to mother as well as baby | D) Nothing active needed since child is asymptomatic
EXPLANATION: Mortality is particularly high in neonates born to susceptible mothers who contract varicella around the time of delivery. Infants whose mothers demonstrate varicella in the period from 5 days prior to delivery to 2 days afterward are at high risk for severe varicella. These are labelled as Neonatal Varicella Syndrome. Because perinatally acquired varicella may be life threatening, the infant should usually be treated with acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 hr IV) when lesions develop. OPTIONS: A) Prophylactic surfactant | B) Early rescue surfactant | C) Late rescue surfactant | D) No role of surfactant since baby is >28 weeks
EXPLANATION: Types of surfactant administration in RDS or suspected RDS in preterm neonates Prophylactic surfactant:- Surfactant is administered within 15-30min of birth, irrespective of the signs of RDS. Prophylactic surfactant therapy should be used with caution as recent evidence suggests an increased risk of BPD/ mortality compared to selective surfactant administration. Late rescue:- Surfactant is administered in an infant with features of RDS after 2 hours. It is usually done in outborn neonates who are transported late to referral centers. OPTIONS: A) Salmonella typhi | B) Parvovirus B19 | C) Streptococcus pneumoniae | D) Parainfluenza virus 1
EXPLANATION: Human Parvovirus B19, also written as B19V, is notorious for aplastic crisis in chronic hemolytic states. ID: 23398 | Answer: [A] Coarctation of aorta
QUESTION: A Child presents with absent femoral pulses with increased blood pressure in the upper limbs and dilated intercostal arteries. Best diagnosis is?
OPTIONS: A) Coarctation of aorta | B) TOF | C) PDA | D) Bicuspid Aortic Valve
EXPLANATION: The clinical picture is compatible with a diagnosis of Coarctation of aorta (CoA). CoA refers to narrowing or constriction in the aorta. The classic sign of coarctation of the aorta is a disparity in pulsation and BP in the arms and legs. The femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis pulses are weak (or absent in up to 40% of patients), in contrast to the bounding pulses of the arms and carotid vessels. The radial and femoral pulses should always be palpated simultaneously for the presence of a radial-femoral delay. Normally, the femoral pulse occurs slightly before the radial pulse. A radio-femoral delay occurs when blood flow to the descending aorta is dependent on collaterals, in which case the femoral pulse is felt after the radial pulse. In normal persons (except neonates), systolic BP in the legs obtained by the cuff method is 10-20 mm Hg higher than that in the arms. In coarctation of the aorta, BP in the legs is lower than that in the arms; frequently, it is difficult to obtain. This differential in BPs is common in patients with coarctation who are older than 1 yr, approximately 90% of whom have systolic hypertension in an upper extremity >95th percentile for age. One site of collateral formation is in the intercostal arteries, which are often dilated and in untreated CoA patients beyond 8-10 years, produces the inferior rib notching sign. Reference: Nelson 21 st Edition, Chapter 454.6

Hemorrhagic disease of the newborn Three forms: early, late, and drug-induced. In older children and adults, vitamin K deficiency may arise from fat malabsorption disorders or the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Prophylaxis with vitamin K supplementation is critical to preventing these conditions, especially in infants . Prevention of Early VKDB: Administer oral or parenteral vitamin K shortly after birth. VKDB due to Maternal Medications: Discontinue maternal medications before delivery, if possible. If discontinuation is not possible, administer vitamin K to the mother. Neonate should receive IM vitamin K immediately after birth. If coagulopathy persists, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) may be needed. High-risk children (e.g., malabsorption): Provide supplemental vitamin K. Monitor prothrombin time (PT) periodically.
