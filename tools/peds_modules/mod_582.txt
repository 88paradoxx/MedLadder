ID: 23433 | Answer: [D] Galactose-1-Phosphate Uridyl Transferase
QUESTION: A neonate delivered via normal vaginal delivery is started on mother's feed. He developed vomiting, hypoglycemia, oil drop cataract, hepatomegaly and mild icterus. Urine was positive for reducing substance, but not glucose. The likely enzyme deficiency is?
OPTIONS: A) Lactase | B) Fructokinase | C) Galactokinase | D) Galactose-1-Phosphate Uridyl Transferase
EXPLANATION: The child is presenting with a crisis, oil drop cataract and non-glucose reducing substances in urine, all after feeding. This suggests a likely diagnosis of Galactosemia. Galactosemia denotes the elevated level of galactose in the blood and is found in 3 distinct inborn errors of galactose metabolism in 1 of the following enzymes: galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase, galactokinase, and uridine diphosphate galactose-4-epimerase. The term galactosemia, generally designates the galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase deficiency, which produces Classic Galactosemia Classic galactosemia is a serious disease with onset of symptoms typically by the 2nd half of the 1st wk of life. The diagnosis should be considered in newborn or young infants with any of the following features within a few days or weeks after birth: jaundice, hepatomegaly, vomiting, hypoglycemia, seizures, lethargy, irritability, feeding difficulties, poor weight gain or failure to regain birthweight, and aminoaciduria. Untreated children may show nuclear oil-drop cataracts, vitreous hemorrhage, hepatic failure, cirrhosis, ascites, splenomegaly, or intellectual disability. Patients with galactosemia are at increased risk for Escherichia coli neonatal sepsis; the onset of sepsis often precedes the diagnosis of galactosemia. Pseudotumor cerebri can occur and cause a bulging fontanel. If untreated, death from liver and kidney failure and sepsis may follow within days. When the diagnosis is not made at birth, damage to the liver (cirrhosis) and brain (intellectual disability) becomes increasingly severe and irreversible. The initial diagnosis of galactosemia is done by demonstration of a reducing substance in several urine specimens collected while the patient is on a diet containing human milk, cow's milk, or any other formula containing lactose. Direct enzyme assay using erythrocytes establishes the diagnosis. Complete withdrawal of lactose from the diet results in improvement of the acute symptoms. ID: 23684 | Answer: [A] Phrynoderma
QUESTION: A child presents with malnutrition and multiple follicular, dry papules on the elbows and knees. Improvement of the skin lesions is observed following vitamin supplementation. What is the most likely diagnosis?
OPTIONS: A) Phrynoderma | B) Keratosis Pilaris | C) Darier disease | D) Lichen planus
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Phrynoderma is a follicular hyperkeratotic disorder associated with multiple vitamin deficiencies, especially vitamin A, and improves with vitamin supplementation. Phrynoderma, also known as toad skin, is a condition characterized by dry, horny follicular papules distributed commonly on extensor surfaces like elbows, knees, buttocks, and thighs. It is seen in malnourished individuals, particularly children, and is attributed to multiple nutritional deficiencies, especially: Vitamin A Vitamin C, Vitamin E, B-complex vitamins, and Essential fatty acids The papules are due to follicular plugging and hyperkeratosis, giving the skin a rough texture. The hallmark of the diagnosis is improvement of the lesions with vitamin and nutritional supplementation. Explanation of other options: Keratosis Pilaris (Option B): Though also present with follicular papules, it is a genetic condition unrelated to malnutrition or vitamin deficiency, and typically does not improve with vitamin supplementation. Darier Disease (Option C): A genetic disorder of keratinization presenting with greasy, warty papules in seborrheic areas (chest, back, forehead); not related to malnutrition and does not improve with vitamins. Lichen Planus (Option D): An autoimmune inflammatory condition with flat-topped, violaceous, itchy papules, often involving wrists and oral mucosa-does not resemble follicular papules or respond to vitamin therapy.

EXPLANATION: Explanation: Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) deficiency causes mucocutaneous lesions, classically presenting as the triad of angular cheilitis, stomatitis, and glossitis . Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin) Deficiency: Explanation: Niacin (Vitamin B3) (Option A): deficiency results in pellagra, which is marked by the 4 D&rsquo;s&mdash;diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia, and, if left untreated, death. ID: 23939 | Answer: [C] MMR vaccine
QUESTION: Which of the following vaccines should not be given to a child with severe combined immunodeficiency disorder (SCID)?
OPTIONS: A) IPV | B) Hepatitis B vaccine | C) MMR vaccine | D) DTaP
EXPLANATION: Explanation: MMR is contraindicated in a child with SCID. Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID) indicates a markedly weakened immune system , so live attenuated vaccines are contraindicated in these children. Live vaccines can replicate in the host and may cause serious infection when immune defenses are impaired. General situations where live vaccines should not be administered include: Primary immunodeficiency disorders Conditions with significant immune suppression , such as: Leukemia or lymphoma Other malignancies Ongoing corticosteroid therapy Treatment with alkylating agents or antimetabolites Therapeutic radiation Pregnancy , because of potential harm to the fetus Explanations for other Options: IPV (Option A): IPV (Inactivated Polio Vaccine) Contains killed poliovirus , not live virus. It is Safe and recommended even in severe immunodeficiency. Hepatitis B vaccine (Option B): A recombinant sub-unit vaccine that uses only the hepatitis B surface antigen. It Cannot cause infection and is safe for immunocompromised children. DTaP (Option D): It Includes inactivated toxoids (diphtheria, tetanus) and acellular pertussis components . It is Entirely non-live, so it is safe to administer in SCID.

ID: 24379 | Answer: [B] A complete blood count with differential
QUESTION: Of the following options, the best initial test for a workup for immunodeficiency is?
OPTIONS: A) Serum levels of IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE | B) A complete blood count with differential | C) Peripheral T-cell phenotyping | D) Erythrocyte sedimentation rate
EXPLANATION: The initial step in the workup of a child with a suspected immunodeficiency should always include a complete blood count and differential, as this test will reveal disorders involving lymphopenia (particularly T-cell lymphopenias) and neutropenia. Other hints can be found by the CBC, including small platelets associated with the Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome, anemias, and evidence of eosinophilia. An erythrocyte sedimentation rate is a nonspecific marker of inflammation and would not point to a specific immunodeficiency. While quantitative immunoglobulins, peripheral T-cell phenotyping, and lack of antibody response to standard immunizations would all be part of an immunodeficiency investigation, these would not be considered first steps in the evaluation. Reference: Nelson textbook of pediatrics, 1 st SA ed., pg. 1004
