ID: 14018 | Answer: [C] Foreign body aspiration
QUESTION: A 2-year-old healthy child suddenly develops breathlessness while playing. What is the most likely cause?
OPTIONS: A) Status asthmaticus | B) Pneumothorax | C) Foreign body aspiration | D) Cardiac failure
EXPLANATION: Foreign body aspiration is highly suspected when a previously healthy, young child suddenly develops breathlessness while playing , as they may have inhaled a small object (toy, food particle) causing airway obstruction. Immediate Symptoms: Sudden onset of respiratory distress, often accompanied by coughing, gagging, or wheezing , is typical of foreign body aspiration. Other Options Considered: Status asthmaticus: Unlikely without a history of asthma or respiratory issues. Pneumothorax: Although it can cause sudden breathlessness, it typically requires trauma or a predisposing lung condition, which is rare in a healthy toddler. Cardiac failure: Sudden onset in a healthy child is rare and would usually involve other symptoms or a predisposing medical history. Key Takeaways: Foreign body aspiration is the most common cause of sudden breathlessness in young, healthy children, especially during play, making it critical to consider in acute respiratory distress. ID: 24053 | Answer: [A] Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)
QUESTION: A child has non-bilious, non-projectile vomiting, around 3 weeks after birth. Abdominal examination was normal but the child has poor weight gain along with feed intolerance. What could be the cause?
OPTIONS: A) Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) | B) Congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (CHPS) | C) Cow milk protein allergy | D) Intestinal obstruction
EXPLANATION: Explanation: A six-week-old infant with poor weight gain, feeding difficulty, and non-bilious, non-projectile vomiting immediately after feeds-while having normal bowel movements and an unremarkable abdominal exam-most likely has gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) . GERD is the backward flow of stomach contents through the lower esophageal sphincter into the esophagus. It is the most common esophageal disorder in childhood and may occur as a normal physiologic process in infants. Typical presentation includes effortless post-meal regurgitation. When reflux causes esophagitis, the infant can show irritability, back-arching, choking, gagging, or refusal to feed, leading to failure to thrive . Some babies also develop airway symptoms such as stridor, obstructive apnea, or lower respiratory issues when reflux aggravates underlying airway conditions like laryngomalacia or bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Most infants outgrow reflux spontaneously by 12-24 months. Management depends on age and symptom severity: avoid overfeeding, burp the infant well during and after feeds, and consider thickened feeds to both increase caloric density and lessen visible regurgitation. Explanations for other Options: Congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis (CHPS)(Option A): Typically causes forceful, projectile non-bilious vomiting beginning at 3&ndash;6 weeks of age and often reveals an olive-shaped mass or visible gastric peristalsis on abdominal exam, findings absent in this child. Cow milk protein allergy (Option C): More often presents with blood-streaked stools, diarrhea, eczema, or vomiting that may be bilious or accompanied by other allergic symptoms, not isolated effortless regurgitation with normal abdominal findings. Intestinal obstruction (Option D): Generally produces bilious vomiting (if distal to the duodenum) or marked abdominal distension and abnormal bowel sounds, which are not seen here.

ID: 24153 | Answer: [A] Focal area of decreased air entry will be suggestive of foreign body
QUESTION: A previously healthy child presents with sudden-onset dyspnea. Chest X-ray reveals unilateral hyperinflation of the lung. Which of the following statements is true for this patient?
OPTIONS: A) Focal area of decreased air entry will be suggestive of foreign body | B) Flexible bronchoscopy used for removal | C) In complete obstruction, ball and valve mechanism causes hyperinflation | D) The child has developed acute laryngotracheobronchitis
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Acute dyspnea in a previously healthy child with unilateral lung hyperinflation on X-ray strongly suggests foreign body aspiration . Auscultation typically shows focal reduction in air entry , which is diagnostic in such cases. Lower Airway Foreign Body Aspiration - Key Points: Epidemiology: Choking is a leading cause of morbidity/mortality in children Most common in toddlers and older infants. Common Aspirated Objects: Food: nuts, seeds, candies, raw fruits/vegetables. Inorganic: coins, pins, balloons, toy parts, magnets. Location: Most often in bronchus > trachea > larynx . Types of Obstruction: Partial obstruction: Ball-valve mechanism &rarr; localized hyperinflation due to air trapping. Complete obstruction: Leads to distal atelectasis, infection, bronchiectasis. Clinical Course: Initial event: coughing, choking, gagging; sometimes spontaneous expulsion. Asymptomatic interval: misleading lull period with lodged foreign body. Later: recurrent cough, fever, hemoptysis, pneumonia, atelectasis. Diagnosis: History of choking event is crucial. Plain X-ray may miss radiolucent objects (80-96%). Expiratory X-ray: asymmetric hyperinflation, air trapping. CT chest: high sensitivity but radiation exposure. Bronchoscopy: definitive diagnostic and therapeutic tool. Treatment: Rigid bronchoscopy is preferred for removal. Antibiotics if secondary infection develops. Explanation of Other Options: Flexible bronchoscopy used for removal (Option B): Flexible bronchoscopy is useful for diagnosis but is not the procedure of choice for removal. Rigid bronchoscopy is preferred for safe and effective extraction of the foreign body. In complete obstruction, ball and valve mechanism causes hyperinflation (Option C): Hyperinflation occurs in partial obstruction due to the ball-valve effect, where air enters during inspiration but cannot exit during expiration. Complete obstruction instead leads to atelectasis and collapse of the distal lung. The child has developed acute laryngotracheobronchitis (Option D): Laryngotracheobronchitis (croup) is a viral illness, often caused by parainfluenza virus, presenting with barking cough, biphasic stridor, and low-grade fever. X-ray shows the "steeple sign" from subglottic narrowing, not unilateral hyperinflation, making this diagnosis unlikely. Key Takeaways: Unilateral hyperinflation in a child = foreign body aspiration until proven otherwise. Auscultation with focal decreased air entry is a diagnostic clue. Rigid bronchoscopy remains the treatment of choice.

ID: 24161 | Answer: [C] Staphylococcus aureus
QUESTION: A child diagnosed with severe pneumonia was started on antibiotics after hospital admission. On day 5 of illness, CXR revealed a well-defined cystic mass in right upper lung, as shown. Most likely etiological agent for pneumonia in this child?
OPTIONS: A) Streptococcus pyogenes | B) Streptococcus pneumoniae | C) Staphylococcus aureus | D) Respiratory Syncytial Virus
EXPLANATION: The image is showing Pneumatocele as a complication of childhood pneumonia. Pneumatoceles are air-filled cavitary lesions usually seen in the lung after infection, trauma, or as a part of more extensive cystic disease of the lung. Most pneumatoceles arise after infection by Staphylococcus aureus., although Streptococci, Hemophilus & Klebsiella can also produce them sometimes. Radiologically they appear as well-defined air-filled cysts in the lung parenchyma. They can sometimes rupture producing pneumothorax. Reference: Nelson 21 st Edition, CPDT

ID: 24162 | Answer: [D] None of the above
QUESTION: The most appropriate investigation in infants with acute bronchiolitis is?
OPTIONS: A) Chest X-ray | B) Arterial blood gas | C) Nasopharyngeal aspirate | D) None of the above
EXPLANATION: Acute Bronchiolitis is a clinical diagnosis in infants who present with coryzal symptoms, wheezes and crepitation. On clinical examination, no other findings can be found except the infant having tachypnoea, mild recession and equal air entry with marked expiratory wheezes and crepitation. There is no need to do a chest X-ray or NPA. NPA is indicated in children with chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease and immunocompromised, progressing neurological problems.
