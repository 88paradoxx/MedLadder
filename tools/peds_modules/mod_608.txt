ID: 4709 | Answer: [C] Transcription of dystrophin mRNA
QUESTION: A 3-year-old child presented with trouble walking to paediatrician. He is diagnosed with DMD (Duchenne muscular dystrophy) by polymerase chain reaction. Genetic analysis shows that the patient's gene for the muscle protein dystrophin on X chromosome contains a mutation. Mutation is found in its promoter region. Which would be the most likely defective process in this child?
OPTIONS: A) Translation of dystrophin protein | B) Post translational modification in dystrophin protein | C) Transcription of dystrophin mRNA | D) Post transcriptional modification in dystrophin mRNA
EXPLANATION: Option A & Option B: Translation is synthesis of protein from mRNA. Mutation in promoter regions does not lead to any problem during translation or post translational modifications. A deficiency of dystrophin mRNA will result in a deficiency in the production of the dystrophin protein. Option C: Mutations in the promoter typically prevent formation of the RNA polymerase II transcription complex, resulting in a decrease in the initiation of mRNA synthesis during transcription. Option D: Post transcriptional modifications like capping, splicing and tailing defects are not a consequence of promoter mutations. ID: 8920 | Answer: [D] No treatment required
QUESTION: A 1-year-old boy is presented with an episode of loss of consciousness followed by jerky movements of his arms and legs for 30 seconds. After this episode, he immediately regained consciousness. The child began crying after his 4-year-old brother snatched a toy from him. The brief shrill cry was followed by a period of expiration; he then turned blue. He had a similar episode 1 week ago when his mother refused to give him ice-cream. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?
OPTIONS: A) IV Lorazepam | B) IM Midazolam | C) Clonazepam | D) No treatment required
EXPLANATION: This patient's episode of cyanosis with loss of consciousness following emotional upset (i.e.,frustration) and vigorous crying is indicative of a breath-holding spell.It typically manifest between the age of 6&ndash;18 months. They are typically triggered by emotional distress, as is the case in this patient. The typical sequence includes crying, exhaling, and holding the breath, followed by cyanosis and loss of consciousness. Patients usually resume breathing and regain consciousness in less than 1 minute. Most importantly, parents should be reassured that the condition is benign and their child will outgrow it by the age of 4&ndash;8 years. The diagnosis is usually based on the clinical features and a detailed description of the episode; no treatment is required. Parents should be aware of the triggers of breath-holding spells, such as tantrums, minor injuries, and distress. However, they should not avoid reprimands for fear of triggering an episode. Since several studies suggest an association between breath-holding spells and iron-deficiency anemia, confirmatory tests (e.g., CBC, serum ferritin) may be considered. Iron supplements may decrease the frequency of breath-holding spells in these children. Reference: Basic & Clinical Pharmacology, Fourteenth Edition By Bertram G. Katzung Page number. 428

ID: 24395 | Answer: [C] CECT Head
QUESTION: A 3-year-old girl presented with fever since 3 days, vomiting, photophobia, neck stiffness and papilledema. You decide to investigate the child before starting therapy. Which among the following is the investigation of choice in this child?
OPTIONS: A) Lumbar Puncture | B) Transcranial USG | C) CECT Head | D) MRI Brain
EXPLANATION: The child has features suggestive of meningitis. Normally, lumbar puncture for CSF analysis is considered the investigation of choice. However, the child is also having papilledema, which suggests a strong possibility of raised ICP. Now, raised ICP is an absolute contraindication for performing LP. Thus, we need to do a non-invasive test, which is sensitive and gives good confirmation of the likelihood of meningitis. Contrast-enhanced CT Scan (CECT) is usually preferred in such cases since it not only delineates meninges inflammation with good sensitivity but can also show dilated ventricles and other sequels associated with raised ICP. Transcranial USG cannot be performed with a good sensitivity in a 3 year old child since the anterior fontanelle will already be closed. MRI Brain is sensitive for picking up inflammation but is time-consuming and can only be done in a stable patient, not in someone with acutely elevated ICP. ID: 24420 | Answer: [D] Juvenile Dermatomyositis
QUESTION: A 4-year-old girl presents with difficulty in going upstairs and leg pains. She is always tired, feels hot and has had some weight loss. The family doctor has seen her on several occasions for eczema that appears on her face and hands. It will disappear and come back within days, even without treatment. EXPLANATION: Juvenile dermatomyositis is the most common inflammatory myopathy in children. It can progress slowly with fatiguability, a low-grade fever and loss of weight, all of which are common. The rash usually appears first over exposed areas, followed by proximal weakness . Periorbital violaceous (heliotropic) erythema occurs across the nasal bridge and around the eyelids. Corticosteroids, methotrexate and cyclosporine can be used in management. Long-term complications are calcification and calcified nodules with cardiomyopathies. KEY TAKEAWAYS: Dermatomyositis is a rare autoimmune disease marked by muscle weakness and a distinctive skin rash, often purple in color, affecting areas like the eyelids and knuckles. It primarily impacts the shoulders, hips, and thighs, making everyday movements difficult. Treatment includes corticosteroids and immunosuppressive drugs to control inflammation and prevent muscle damage. ID: 24579 | Answer: [B] Peripheral blood film
QUESTION: A child presented with proptosis. You suspect it to be a chloroma. Next best investigation to make the diagnosis (among the following) is?
OPTIONS: A) Platelet count | B) Peripheral blood film | C) TLC/WBC Count | D) Haemoglobin
EXPLANATION: Chloroma, also known as granulocytic sarcoma , is a rare extramedullary manifestation of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) . It can present as proptosis when the orbit is involved. The best investigation to make the diagnosis of chloroma is a peripheral blood film , which helps identify the presence of myeloblasts . These cells are characteristic of AML and would confirm the diagnosis. In addition, chloromas are often associated with specific cytogenetic abnormalities, such as t(8;21) translocation. While a bone marrow aspirate and flow cytometry are essential for definitive diagnosis, a peripheral blood smear is a practical, quick test to support the diagnosis. AML patients may also present with unusual symptoms such as "blueberry muffin" lesions , subcutaneous nodules, or infiltration of the gingiva, which can be helpful in diagnosing chloromas. Incorrect Options: Platelet count: Platelet counts may show thrombocytopenia in AML, but this is nonspecific and does not aid in the diagnosis of chloromas. TLC/WBC Count: Total leukocyte count may show leukocytosis , but it does not provide specific information about the presence of chloromas or myeloblasts, making it insufficient for diagnosis. Hemoglobin: Anemia is common in AML due to bone marrow failure, but hemoglobin levels are not specific for diagnosing chloromas or AML. Key Takeaway: Peripheral blood film is the best initial test to identify myeloblasts when chloroma is suspected. Definitive diagnosis requires bone marrow aspirate , flow cytometry , and cytogenetic analysis . Chloromas are extramedullary AML manifestations and can cause symptoms such as proptosis, "blueberry muffin" lesions , and subcutaneous nodules in infants.
