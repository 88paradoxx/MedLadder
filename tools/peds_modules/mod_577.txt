ID: 17381 | Answer: [A] DMD
QUESTION: Dystrophin absent and presence of small muscle fibrils cause?
OPTIONS: A) DMD | B) Becker muscle dystrophy | C) Myotonic dystrophy | D) None
EXPLANATION: Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophy are the Dystrophinopaties. DMD is characterized by absent dystrophin and Becker's muscular dystrophy is characterized by either decreased level or size of dystrophin. Total absence of dystrophin points to the diagnosis of DMD. Biopsy is not diagnostic in myopathies. So the microscopy will be almost the same for DMD and BMD. Muscle biopsy in young ages will show degeneration and regeneration of muscle fibres with preserved architecture. There is no active inflammation except for myophagocytosis. As the disease progresses, the muscle fibers are replaced by fatty infiltration. The remaining muscle fibers show varying size from atrophic to hypertrophic. Immunohistochemical staining shows absence of sarcolemmal staining in DMD and reduced staining in BMD. They are both XL recessive inheritance. Dystrophin provides stability to the muscle fibre by acting as a bridge between the cytoskeleton inside the muscle fibre and the basement membrane outside the cell. DMD results from deletions or frameshift mutations in the dystrophin gene, leading to total absence of the protein, whereas BMD results in the formation of truncated variant of dystrophin that leads to partially functional protein.

ID: 18570 | Answer: [A] Periventricular leukomalacia
QUESTION: A patient presents with spastic quadriplegia. What is the most likely site of lesion?
OPTIONS: A) Periventricular leukomalacia | B) Basal ganglia calcification | C) Cerebellar degeneration | D) Cystic encephalomalacia
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Periventricular leukomalacia (PVL) refers to ischemic necrosis of the white matter adjacent to the lateral ventricles, typically seen in preterm infants due to hypoxic-ischemic injury . Gait becomes wide-based and unsteady . No hyperreflexia or spastic quadriplegia. Seen in chronic alcoholism , paraneoplastic syndromes , or vitamin E deficiency . Cystic encephalomalacia (Option D): Post-hypoxic destruction of brain parenchyma forming cystic cavities . Causes focal neurological deficits depending on area affected. Usually results in seizures or cognitive delay , not pure spasticity. Common sequel of severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.

ID: 23415 | Answer: [D] Exstrophy bladder
QUESTION: Which neonatal anomaly is depicted in the image?
OPTIONS: A) Omphalocele | B) Gastroschisis | C) Patent vitello intestinal duct | D) Exstrophy bladder
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Exstrophy bladder is a congenital anomaly where the bladder mucosa is exposed outside the abdominal wall due to failure of mesodermal migration. Bladder exstrophy results from a failure of mesoderm to migrate and form the lower abdominal wall, leading to a defect through which the posterior bladder wall protrudes and is visible externally. It typically presents in neonates with the bladder mucosa exposed, downwardly displaced umbilicus, widely separated pubic symphysis, and epispadias. In males, dorsal chordee and shortened penis are seen. In females, there is bifid clitoris and wide labial separation. If left untreated, patients suffer from complete urinary incontinence and are at increased risk for bladder adenocarcinoma. This condition is easily distinguishable from other anterior abdominal wall defects due to visible bladder mucosa, absence of a herniated bowel sac, and association with genital anomalies. Explanation of other options: Omphalocele (Option A): Presents as herniation of abdominal contents into the base of the umbilical cord, covered by a translucent sac. The umbilical cord inserts into the sac-not seen in exstrophy. Gastroschisis (Option B): Abdominal wall defect lateral to the umbilicus, with free-floating bowel loops that lack a covering sac-distinct from bladder mucosa exposure. Patent vitello intestinal duct (Option C): Persistent communication between the intestine and umbilicus, may present as a fistula, sinus, or cyst near the umbilicus-does not show an exposed bladder.

ID: 23449 | Answer: [C] Initiate bag-and-mask ventilation
QUESTION: A full-term infant born through emergency cesarean section to a 34-year-old mother whose pregnancy was complicated by hypertension and abnormal fetal heart monitoring. At delivery she is limp, apneic, and bradycardic. Which of the following is the best first step in resuscitation?
OPTIONS: A) Administer IV bicarbonate | B) Only chest compressions without PPV | C) Initiate bag-and-mask ventilation | D) Intubate with an endotracheal tube and suction meconium from the trachea
EXPLANATION: In a baby born through meconium-stained liquor (MSL), we evaluate if the baby is vigorous or not. In a vigorous baby, only routine care is recommended, while in a non-vigorous baby, we perform the usual initial steps and immediately start with positive-pressure ventilation (PPV). Previously, the presence of meconium-stained amniotic fluid in a non-vigorous infant required tracheal intubation to attempt to aspirate meconium below the cords. Recent evidence has shown that this has no benefit and actually increases morbidity and mortality owing to delayed initiation of PPV. Bag and mask ventilation to administer PPV is not contraindicated anymore. There is no role of intrapartum suctioning or tracheal suctioning in neonates born through MSL anymore. If HR does not improve, the next step is to check for chest rise , and if chest rise is inadequate, corrective ventilation measures should be taken (e.g., adjusting the airway or increasing ventilation pressure). QUESTION: When is a blood sample taken for diagnosing neonatal hypothyroidism?
OPTIONS: A) Immediately from the umbilical cord | B) 24 hours after delivery | C) 23-48 hours after delivery | D) 72 hours after delivery
EXPLANATION: Explanation: Serum TSH levels are typically measured between 2 to 4 days of life for the screening of congenital hypothyroidism . This timing accounts for the physiological TSH surge that occurs within the first 48 hours after birth , which can lead to false-positive results if testing is done too early. Explanation of other options: Immediately from the umbilical cord (Option A): Cord blood TSH is not considered reliable for screening congenital hypothyroidism , as the physiological TSH surge typically occurs after birth . Testing with cord blood may miss elevated TSH levels , leading to false-negative results . Therefore, TSH measurement is recommended between 2 to 4 days of life for accurate detection. 24 hours after delivery (Option B): TSH levels are still rising due to the physiological TSH surge , leading to false positives . 23-48 hours after delivery (Option C): While better than testing immediately, TSH levels may still be fluctuating , increasing the risk of false positives . Key Takeaways: Neonatal hypothyroidism screening is best performed at 72 hours (2-4 days) after birth to avoid the transient TSH surge and obtain accurate results.
