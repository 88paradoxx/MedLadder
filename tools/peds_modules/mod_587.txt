ID: 23354 | Answer: [A] Diazepam
QUESTION: A 2-year-old child is brought to the emergency room with a generalised seizure following a high-grade fever. What is the first-line drug of choice for seizure control in this setting?
OPTIONS: A) Diazepam | B) Ethosuximide | C) Valproate | D) Phenytoin
EXPLANATION: Explanation: The child is suffering from febrile seizures , and among the given options, diazepam is the first-line drug of choice for seizure control for this condition. Febrile seizures are convulsions seen in children between 6 and 60 months , most often at 12-18 months, occurring with fever &ge;38&deg;C but without CNS infection or metabolic disturbance. Simple febrile seizures are brief, generalised tonic-clonic episodes lasting under 15 minutes and not recurring within 24 hours, while complex febrile seizures are longer than 15 minutes, focal, or recurrent. Febrile status epilepticus lasts over 30 minutes. Treatment does not involve routine, continuous antiepileptic therapy for simple febrile seizures. Acute seizures lasting beyond 5 minutes may be treated with lorazepam , midazolam, or diazepam, and intermittent therapy with oral or rectal benzodiazepines may be used in recurrent cases. Long-term continuous antiepileptic drug use is discouraged due to risks outweighing benefits. Antipyretics reduce discomfort but do not prevent recurrence, while iron deficiency increases risk. Explanation of other options: Ethosuximide (Option B): Ethosuximide is a calcium channel blocker used in absence seizures . Valproate (Option C): Valproate is a broad-spectrum antiseizure drug used to manage absence , myoclonic, focal, and tonic-clonic seizures. Phenytoin (Option D): Phenytoin is a widely used antiseizure drug effective for focal and generalised tonic-clonic seizures, including focal to bilateral tonic-clonic and tonic-clonic of unknown onset, but it is not useful for absence seizures. Beyond epilepsy, phenytoin may help in trigeminal and related neuralgias, though carbamazepine is often preferred, and it is also used in managing certain cardiac arrhythmias.

QUESTION: A child presents with nausea and abdominal pain following an iron tablet overdose. What is the specific antidote used to treat this condition?
OPTIONS: A) EDTA | B) Deferoxamine | C) British anti-Lewisite | D) Penicillamine
EXPLANATION: Explanation: The presentation is consistent with acute iron poisoning . The antidote of choice is deferoxamine , a specific iron chelating agent, particularly effective in children. It is administered as an IV infusion at a dose of 15 mg/kg/hr until serum iron levels fall below 300 &mu;g/dl or for up to 24 hours after the child stops excreting the characteristic "vin rose" colored urine. Explanation of Other Options: EDTA (Option A): Used primarily for lead poisoning in both adults and children. It has a high affinity for lead but does not effectively bind iron. The lead-EDTA complex is excreted in urine. British anti-Lewisite (BAL) (Option C): Effective against arsenic, gold, copper, and mercury toxicity. It works by forming stable complexes with these metals, but has no role in iron poisoning. BAL is given parenterally and requires careful monitoring due to adverse effects. Penicillamine (Option D): Mainly indicated in Wilson's disease and cystinuria . It may be used for lead poisoning in some cases, but it is not effective in acute iron overdose. Key takeaway: Excessive ingestion of ferrous sulfate tablets can result in acute iron poisoning , which typically progresses through the following stages: Gastrointestinal toxicity (vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea, hematemesis) A latent phase of apparent improvement lasting up to 48 hours Development of circulatory shock , metabolic acidosis , and myocardial dysfunction Gastrointestinal symptoms usually appear at ingested doses of 15-30 mg/kg Management: Gastric decontamination with gastric lavage using sodium bicarbonate Correction of acidosis with IV sodium bicarbonate (3 ml/kg, diluted twice with 5% dextrose) Fluid resuscitation as needed Chelation therapy with IV deferoxamine (15 mg/kg/hr) until serum iron is reduced to "vin rose" urine excretion Dialysis may be required in cases of renal failure to eliminate deferoxamine-iron complexes

ID: 23895 | Answer: [C] Adenosine 0.1 mg/kg
QUESTION: A 2-year-old child presents with stable vitals and an ECG showing paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT). Which of the following is the appropriate treatment?
OPTIONS: A) Cardioversion | B) Adenosine 0.2 mg/kg | C) Adenosine 0.1 mg/kg | D) IV Adrenaline
EXPLANATION: Explanation: In a hemodynamically stable child with PSVT, the first-line treatment is IV adenosine at an initial dose of 0.1 mg/kg. Paroxysmal supraventricular Tachycardia (PSVT) is a common pediatric arrhythmia characterized by a sudden onset of narrow complex tachycardia. In children with stable hemodynamics, adenosine is the drug of choice due to its short half-life and effectiveness in blocking AV nodal conduction, thereby terminating the reentrant circuit. The initial dose of adenosine is 0.1 mg/kg IV as a rapid bolus. If ineffective, the dose can be increased to 0.2 mg/kg. It must be followed by a saline flush and should be given via a central line or proximal IV for maximal effect. The drug works by causing transient AV block, which often restores sinus rhythm in AVNRT or AVRT. Before adenosine, vagal maneuvers (like Valsalva or ice pack to the face in infants) are attempted. ID: 24182 | Answer: [A] Hydroxyurea
QUESTION: In Sickle Cell Anemia, which drug is used to decrease the incidence of painful vaso-occlusive crisis?
OPTIONS: A) Hydroxyurea | B) Methotrexate-Leucovorin | C) Eltrombopag | D) Azathioprine
EXPLANATION: To decrease Vasoocclusive crisis in SCA pt MC drug used:Hydroxyurea increases production of HbF New Drugs: Voxelotor & Crizanlizumab decrease RBC polymerisation Hydroxyurea is the primary medication used to decrease the incidence of painful vaso-occlusive crises in patients with Sickle Cell Anemia (SCA) . This drug works by increasing the production of fetal hemoglobin (HbF) , which reduces the sickling of red blood cells, thereby decreasing the frequency and severity of vaso-occlusive episodes. Mechanism of Action: Hydroxyurea works by increasing HbF production, which inhibits sickling of red blood cells. HbF does not participate in the polymerization process that causes sickle-shaped cells, thus reducing the tendency of sickle cells to clump together and block blood vessels (leading to vaso-occlusion). Other Options: Methotrexate-Leucovorin : Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant and is typically used for conditions like autoimmune disorders and certain cancers. Leucovorin is used to mitigate the toxic effects of methotrexate but has no role in Sickle Cell Anemia management. Eltrombopag : This drug is a thrombopoietin receptor agonist used in the treatment of thrombocytopenia (low platelet counts) but is not used in the management of Sickle Cell Anemia. Azathioprine : Azathioprine is an immunosuppressive drug used primarily for organ transplant rejection or autoimmune conditions. It has no role in decreasing the frequency of vaso-occlusive crises in Sickle Cell Anemia. Additional New Drugs in Sickle Cell Anemia: Voxelotor : This drug works by increasing hemoglobin's affinity for oxygen, which reduces the polymerization of sickle hemoglobin. Crizanlizumab : This medication is a monoclonal antibody that works by preventing the adhesion of sickle cells to the blood vessel walls, thereby reducing the occurrence of vaso-occlusive crises. Key Takeaway: Hydroxyurea is the mainstay of treatment to prevent painful vaso-occlusive crises in Sickle Cell Anemia by increasing HbF levels, while newer drugs like Voxelotor and Crizanlizumab also show promise in reducing sickling and preventing crises.
